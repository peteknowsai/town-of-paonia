Paonia, Colorado · July 2026

The Story of Paonia's Money

Why the budget says the town is spending more than it earns, why the new audit says everything is fine, and why both are telling the truth. The story of how a small Colorado town moved its growing cost of government out of the budget its voters watch and into the water bills its ratepayers pay.

Prepared July 31, 2026 · Sources: FY-2026 Adopted Budget · FY-2025 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (audited by Hinkle & Co., presented July 28, 2026) · Board meeting records

Chapter One

A town's money lives in buckets, and the buckets have rules

Forget everything about corporate accounting. A town does not have one bank account with one balance. It has several separate funds, and the law treats each one like a bucket with its own rules about what can go in and what can come out. Paonia's buckets look like this:

Spend on anything

General Fund

The town's checking account. Police, parks, administration, day-to-day operations. Fed by 2% of the 3% sales tax, a small property tax, and fees.

Capital only

Sales Tax Capital Improvement Fund

The third percentage point of sales tax, added by voters in 2018. Legally locked to capital projects. Cannot pay a salary or keep the lights on.

Ratepayer businesses

Water · Sewer · Trash

Three self-supporting "enterprise funds." Your utility bill is their only income. They carry the town's debt, and that debt is repaid by rates.

Two rules from this picture drive everything that follows:

Rule one: restricted money can't rescue operations. The town can have cash in the capital fund and still be unable to make payroll from it. "The town has money" and "the General Fund has money" are different sentences.

Rule two: where a cost is charged is a choice. When the town administrator spends half their week on water projects, the town may charge part of that salary to the Water Fund instead of the General Fund. That's legal and often correct. But it means a fund's "spending" can rise or fall without a single dollar of actual activity changing — only the label on it. That rule is the key to the whole story.

Chapter Two

The decade the town staffed up, and the year the money stopped

In 2013, Paonia was not a well-run town. That year's audit records a $252,053 employee fraud loss, no ledger of what the town even owned, and total townwide payroll of roughly $938,000. Over the following decade the town professionalized: real policies, clean audits, grant-writing capacity that landed millions in infrastructure money. It paid for that with people. Townwide personnel cost is now roughly $2.3 million — about two and a half times 2013, for the same 1,450 residents.

That growth was affordable because revenue grew too: General Fund revenue rose about 90% from 2013 to 2024. Then it stopped. Sales tax has flattened. Cannabis tax revenue is declining. And the town's 2020 water tap moratorium means no new homes or businesses can connect, so the town's tax base physically cannot grow — a constraint the town's own budget documents acknowledge. Meanwhile payroll compounds every year through cost-of-living raises and health insurance increases whether or not revenue moves.

One more structural fact most people miss: when voters raised the sales tax from 2% to 3% in 2018, every dollar of the increase went to the locked capital bucket. The General Fund — the fund that pays for police and staff — still runs on the same 2% it ran on in 2013.

Chapter Three

The town admitted the problem in writing

You don't have to take an outsider's word that there's a gap. The town's own adopted budget for 2026 says it, in one remarkable sentence:

"The anticipated decrease in the General Fund balance for FY-2026 reflects the use of unrestricted, unassigned fund balance to support operating expenditures that exceed projected revenues rather than for capital outlays." — Town of Paonia, FY-2026 Adopted Budget

Translated: we plan to spend more on daily operations than we take in, and we will cover the difference from savings — and no, it's not because of one-time construction projects. The numbers behind that sentence: about $1.66 million of expected revenue against $1.76 million of planned spending, a roughly $102,000 draw on reserves. The town is budgeting to spend about $1.06 for every $1.00 it earns. Accountants call this a structural deficit: the gap isn't caused by a bad year or a one-time purchase, it's built into the shape of the budget — and with costs compounding and revenue frozen, it widens on its own every year nobody acts.

Chapter Four

Then the audit arrived, and it sounds like a different town

On July 28 the Board received the audited financial report for 2025. Its headlines: the General Fund grew by $116,574. Every governmental fund grew. The town's overall net position jumped $4.2 million. The auditor issued an "unmodified opinion" — a clean audit, the best grade there is — with no material weaknesses, no adjustments, no disagreements with management.

So which is it? A town writing down that it can't cover its own operations, or a town whose reserves just grew and whose auditor found nothing wrong?

Both. Understanding why both are true is the whole story.

Chapter Five

What a clean audit actually certifies (and what it doesn't)

This is the single most misunderstood thing in municipal finance, so it's worth being precise. An audit answers one question: do these financial statements honestly describe what happened? It does not answer: was what happened wise? Is the town financially healthy? Is the trend sustainable?

A clean opinion means the bookkeeping is honest. A town can be honestly drifting toward a fiscal cliff and collect clean audits the entire way down — every one of them deserved. When officials say "the audit was clean, so the finances are fine," the precise reading is: the audit says the numbers are honest. What follows is what the honest numbers say.

Chapter Six

Where the 2025 "surplus" actually came from

Here's where Rule Two from Chapter One earns its keep. The audit's own narrative — management's discussion, in the town's own words — explains the $116,574 improvement. Three things produced it, and none of them is new money.

First, and biggest: costs were moved to other buckets, not reduced. General Fund spending fell $253,845 in 2025. The audit states plainly that this "does not indicate a broad reduction in municipal services." Instead, the town changed its allocation methodology — the formula for deciding which fund gets charged for shared staff. Administrative salaries, benefits, and Board costs that used to hit the General Fund were charged to the Water, Sewer, and Trash funds. The "General Government" line alone fell from $443,549 to $103,249 — a 76.7% drop in one year, with no one laid off and nothing discontinued. Eligible capital costs were similarly reclassified into the locked capital fund.

Second: interest earnings. The General Fund collected $190,453 in investment income in 2025 — nearly 11% of all its revenue — because interest rates were high. The audit itself notes the Federal Reserve cut rates three times in late 2025 and that this income will shrink. It is a windfall, not a revenue source the town controls.

Third, for the flashy "$4.2 million" headline: a one-time gift. $3 million of it is the forgiveness of water-related debt — genuinely good news, but a single event in the utility funds. It says nothing about operations, and it will never happen again.

Meanwhile the revenue side — the side that would signal an actual turnaround — was flat: up $3,471 on $1.76 million. Two-tenths of one percent.

The arithmetic nobody has said out loud

Reported 2025 General Fund result+ $116,574
Remove the admin costs shifted to the utility funds (the allocation change, per the audit's own General Government figures)− $340,300
2025 result under the old methodology, roughly− $223,726

In other words: measured the same way as 2024, the year 2025 was a deficit year — a larger one than 2024's $140,742. The improvement is real on paper and legitimate in method, but it came from relabeling costs, not from earning more or spending less.

To be precise about what this is and is not: allocating shared costs to the utilities is a defensible, arguably more accurate practice. The utilities really do consume administrative time, and the old method may have overcharged the General Fund for years. The point is not that anyone cooked the books — the auditor found the statements fairly presented, and there's no reason to doubt that. The point is that a methodology choice, made by the outgoing administrator in his final year, produced the entire turnaround in the town's most-watched number — and that choice has never been presented to the public as a choice, with its assumptions on the table.

How the swing actually happened. The paper trail across four budget cycles shows this was engineered in stages, not discovered in one audit:

December 12, 2023 — one meeting, two resolutions, 27 minutes apart. At 7:17 PM the Board takes up Resolution 20-2023: the five-year water rate increases, presented as the path to loan qualification and infrastructure. At 7:44 PM the same meeting adopts Resolution 19-2023: the FY-2024 budget — which quietly creates a "Trustee/Admin Salaries" line of $40,750 in the Water Fund and another in the Sewer Fund. Those lines had been zero in 2022 and zero in 2023. The same budget cut the General Fund's administrator line from $153,114 (2023 actual, a transition year) to $44,500. The minutes record questions about land-acknowledgment wording, a coding error, and generators. The new utility admin lines were never mentioned. Trustees praised "a transparent and easy to understand budget."

From there the glide continued: the FY-2025 budget (adopted December 10, 2024 — hearing discussion: typos, grants, short-term-rental revenue) held the structure at $39,000 GF / $40,500 + $40,500 utilities. During 2025 the "fixed allocation methodology" was revised again in the ledger — by June 2025 the monthly reports already show the deeper spread — and the original 2025 budget the Board had adopted still showed General Government at $371,100, so the collapse to the reported figure happened in actuals, not in anything the Board voted on. Then in spring 2026 the departing Administrator/Treasurer and the contract accountants wrote the ACFR narrative presenting it as "more accurate allocation." By the FY-2026 budget: $33,000 GF against $48,600 + $48,600 — the 25/75 split.

Two facts about that final narration deserve their own sentence. First, the same audited document reports 2025 General Government spending three different ways: $103,249 in management's narrative, $291,008 in the budget-comparison schedule, $267,515 in the government-wide statement. Second, the published document still contains an unedited drafting note — “The chart title should read ‘Business-Type Activities Ending Net Position by Utility’…” — an editor’s comment to themselves, left in the final audited report. Third, the auditor’s engagement letter states the required supplementary information — exactly where the budget comparison and management's narrative live — was not audited and receives no opinion. The clean opinion covers the financial statements; the story told about them belongs to management alone.

Chapter Seven

The costs didn't disappear. They moved to the water bill.

Every dollar of overhead shifted out of the General Fund landed in the Water, Sewer, and Trash funds. Those funds have exactly one source of income: utility bills. And they are the most stressed part of the town's finances:

They are in the middle of the largest debt expansion in town history — the Western Loop waterline (~$5.2M) and the 2-million-gallon tank project (~$3M), with two brand-new state loans added in 2025. The debt is repaid entirely by ratepayers, on a customer base that legally cannot grow while the tap moratorium stands, in a town whose median household income ($53,646) is low enough that the state classifies it as a Disadvantaged Community. The town has already committed, in writing, to years of rate increases to carry this debt.

So the reallocation has a consequence beyond optics: it makes the General Fund look healthier by adding weight to the funds least able to carry it. The same administrative payroll that was straining the tax-funded budget is now pressing on the rate-funded one. If one sentence in this document matters most, it is this one.

The town's own 2026 budget lines show exactly how far this goes. The General Fund's "Town Admin" line is $33,000. The Water Fund and Sewer Fund each carry a "Trustees/Admin Salaries" line of $48,600. Of the $130,200 across those three lines, the General Fund pays 25.4% — taxpayers fund a quarter of the town's administration; water and sewer ratepayers fund the other three-quarters. The Trash Fund has no admin line at all, but its wage line roughly tripled between 2024 and 2025. Even the elected officials moved: the General Fund's mayor-and-trustees line was cut to $2,400 while trustee costs ride inside the utility "Trustees/Admin" lines — meaning the Board's own compensation is now majority ratepayer-funded, which is the hardest piece of this to defend, because the Board governs everything, not just the pipes.

And the rate timeline makes the sequencing plain. Every current rate increase was adopted before the cost shift: trash in 2022 (Res. 2022-14), the five-year water step plan in December 2023 (Res. 2023-20, sold as necessary to qualify for state loans), sewer in 2023 (Ord. 2023-07, sold as reserves for treatment requirements). Ratepayers were told those increases were for infrastructure and debt. Then in 2025 the administrative payroll moved into those same funds — with no new rate action, no public statement connecting the two, and no quantification anywhere of how much moved. The money to carry it comes out of margin that was priced for pipes.

Is a big utility share of the administrator's time plausible? For the current interim, honestly, yes — his own written reports are dominated by the Western Loop, the tank closeout, and metering projects. But notice what that concedes: the town's chief executive is functionally a utility projects manager, which is the strongest argument yet that the three-in-one job should be unbundled. What's not plausible is that this was measured. The audit's own word for the method is a "fixed allocation methodology" — fixed percentages set by management, "based on the services provided to each fund." No timesheets, no time study, no Board vote appears in any published record. The percentages are a judgment call, made by the administration whose costs were being judged.

There's a representation problem buried in this too. The water system serves customers outside town limits — people who cannot vote in Town elections. The legal deal that makes it legitimate to charge non-voters at all is that rates reflect the actual cost of their service. The reallocation strains that deal: out-of-town customers now fund three-quarters of the administrator's cost and a share of the Trustees' own compensation through their water bills, under a formula nobody has published. And the audit itself leans on these customers when convenient — its debt statistics (utility debt tripled to $9.67M in 2025; debt per capita $6,529) carry a repeated caveat that the ratios "overstate the relative burden on in-town taxpayers" because the service area is bigger than the voter base. The same document that uses out-of-town ratepayers to soften the debt optics routed the town's overhead into their bills.

Hotchkiss handles the identical problem the transparent way. In Hotchkiss's 2026 budget, every salary lives in the General Fund — including the administrator's — and the utilities reimburse the General Fund through single, visible transfer lines anyone can read: Water Fund "Transfer to General Fund" $187,267; Sanitation Fund "Trans to Gen – Labor & Benefit" $93,634; Capital Projects Fund labor transfer $93,634. Same economics — utilities pay for the labor they use — but the subsidy is one auditable number the Board adopts in public, not an internal formula that must be reverse-engineered from a 76.7% year-over-year swing. Hotchkiss even budgeted a three-year salary and wage survey ($25,000) to document what its people cost. The fix is not exotic: charge the utilities the way the town eleven miles down the road does — one published line, adopted in public.

And Hotchkiss isn't the exception — it's the region. A survey of five more Western Slope town budgets shows every model in use, and none looks like Paonia's. Norwood (population ~550, a third of Paonia's size) budgets its administrator at $85,000, entirely in the General Fund, as a single named line anyone can read. Palisade carries its administration wages ($258,000–$320,000) and its trustees' wages ($18,000) in the General Fund. Ridgway budgets ~$780,000 of administrative wages in its General Fund. Two towns do allocate across utility funds — and both do it transparently: Crawford splits clerk payroll 50% General Fund / 25% water / 25% sewer in identical labeled lines, and Dolores books "Administrative Salaries" of $71,511 in equal thirds across its General, Water, and Sewer funds — same line name, same amount, visible in each fund. So the practice of sharing admin costs with utilities exists. What exists nowhere else in the survey: a General Fund share as low as 25%, trustee compensation riding in utility funds, or an allocation that swung by three-quarters in a single year without a published formula. Paonia's $33,000 General Fund administrator line is the smallest of any town surveyed — including towns a third its size.

And the practice has a name in Colorado law. The Colorado Supreme Court's test is simple: a tax raises revenue "to defray the general expenses of government," while a fee "defrays the cost of a particular government service" (Barber v. Ritter, 196 P.3d 238 (Colo. 2008)). In Bloom v. City of Fort Collins, 784 P.2d 304 (Colo. 1989), the Court examined a Fort Collins utility fee whose ordinance allowed revenues to be moved to other city funds — and held that using fee revenue that way "would be tantamount to requiring the class of persons responsible for the fee… to bear a disproportionate share of the burden of providing revenues to defray general governmental expenses unrelated to the purpose for which the fee is imposed. The effect of such a transfer would be to render the [fee] the functional equivalent of a tax." Under TABOR, a tax requires a vote. No Paonia voter has ever been asked.

To be clear about what the law does and does not say: charging a utility fund a genuine, demonstrated share of an administrator's time is legitimate, expected, and practiced statewide — Colorado Springs, Loveland, and Longmont all move utility money toward general government, and every one of them publishes the percentage on its rate page or in its charter formula, where ratepayers can see it and argue about it. The professional standard is equally specific: the Government Finance Officers Association calls for a written, documented, annually reviewed cost allocation plan whose basis is "fair, rational, and consistently applied" and "reasonably approximates the proportional share of service received." An allocation that exists only as a percentage in a spreadsheet is not a cost allocation plan. It is a number. And Colorado's Local Government Budget Law (C.R.S. 29-1-109) adds a hard requirement wherever money moves between funds by transfer: it must be done by resolution, with the amounts "set forth in full" and "documented in detail in the minutes." No such resolution appears in any Paonia minutes reviewed for this report.

Chapter Eight

The signature on the report, and the job nobody holds anymore

The audited report was prepared and signed by Stefen Wynn, in his combined role as Town Administrator and Treasurer — dated July 12, 2026, the final day of his contract. For years, one person authored the budget, executed the budget, decided the cost-allocation formulas between funds, and then signed the annual report attesting to the results. No checks failed, and the audits were clean. But there was no independent set of eyes inside the building.

The town's new leadership has already said the quiet part. When the Board appointed Interim Administrator Greg Sund as Treasurer on June 23 — continuing the same combined role as a stopgap — he told the Board, on camera, exactly what he expected and what he wants done about it:

"One thing I'd like you to really consider is: I'm kind of expecting the audit report to include separation of duties to a greater extent than exists today. And so maybe in the 2027 budget, I will probably put in there that you consider adding another position to the accounting office… so that you can better separate duties instead of having one person do most all of the work. I just think that's better business." — Interim Administrator Greg Sund, June 23, 2026 meeting, on accepting the Treasurer appointment (video, 1:36:36)

Asked what the role is called, he answered: "They call it finance director… there are times I think we shot too much work at that one single person. That's really not good."

Then note what happened next. The audit came back on July 28 and said nothing about separation of duties. No material weakness, no recommendation, no management letter comment. The prediction from the town's own finance overseer simply didn't surface. That's not a contradiction either — auditors are only required to report control problems that rise to a formal threshold, and small-town audits routinely stay silent below it. But it means the org-chart problem is now documented nowhere except Sund's own words and his forthcoming 2027 budget, which his July 28 report confirms he has already started drafting. (When a resident asked him at the July 28 meeting to confirm what he'd said, his answer never made it onto the recording's microphones.)

A clean audit and a call for a real treasurer are not in tension. The audit checks the books; it does not check the org chart. Auditors themselves preach separation of duties — the principle that the person who spends money, the person who records it, and the person who reports on it should not be the same person — as the most basic safeguard in public finance. Paonia hasn't had it. With the administrator's chair now empty (and the mayor's too, as of July 14), the town is about to decide what the next administration looks like. This is the moment that decision is live.

Chapter Nine

Where the money is going instead

The rate increases were adopted for one stated purpose: infrastructure. Watch what actually happened to the infrastructure, and what happened to the spending.

The infrastructure keeps slipping. The 2025 budget appropriated $5.5 million of Water Fund capital work; $1.75 million was spent. The General Fund budgeted $2.86 million of capital outlay; the audited budget schedule records $31,588. Grant revenue was budgeted at $1.56 million; $561,478 arrived. The Western Loop transmission line — the flagship project the rate plan was built around — received one bid in 2026, which staff recommended rejecting in July, with 5 of 28 required private easements in hand. The two-million-gallon tank project finished late, with the temporary water system running past its $300,000 estimate and roughly $39,000 in liquidated damages charged to the contractor at closeout.

The spending arrives on schedule anyway. The 2026 budget carries roughly $616,000 of equipment and metering purchases across the funds: $70,600 for police equipment, $45,000 for parks, $43,000 for streets, $150,000 of Water Fund machinery plus a $250,000 raw-water metering project, $22,350 for sewer, $20,500 for trash — plus four new vehicles, one for police and three charged to the Water Fund. Meanwhile the payroll base grew from 23 to 25 full-time positions, every employee received a minimum 5.7% cost-of-living raise going into 2025, and the town absorbed a 13% health-insurance increase — all of it now majority-charged, through the allocation formula, to the utility funds.

Some of it is being bought twice. The town installed a Verkada surveillance camera system in October 2025. By April 28, 2026, the Board had disabled the public-facing cameras; on May 12, 2026 it voted to terminate the contract and remove them; and on June 9, 2026 it approved purchasing a replacement Reolink system. Two camera systems inside nine months, the first now coming off the poles.

And the Trash Fund is the canary. The same maneuver that produced the General Fund "surplus" broke the smallest fund in the same year. In 2025 the Trash Fund collected $342,480 from customers and spent $169,259 on salaries and $52,642 on benefits — 65 cents of every ratepayer dollar going to payroll — against roughly $75,000 of actual direct service cost (landfill fees, the truck, supplies, repairs). The salary line tells the allocation story in miniature: $127,400 in 2022, $159,949 in 2023, budgeted down to $93,000 in 2024, then budgeted at $144,000 for 2025 and amended upward mid-year to $168,960 as the allocation deepened. The fund posted a $42,314 loss and its reserves fell to $309,183. Water and Sewer absorb their share of the shifted overhead only because their rate increases are larger; Trash's rate base was too small to hide it, so the deficit surfaced there first. The 2026 budget projects the Trash Fund's balance falling another 27%.

Even the bill for checking the books rides the formula. Hinkle & Company's 2024 invoice for auditing the town's financial statements — $16,600 — was split $2,988 to the General Fund and $13,612 to Water, Sewer and Trash: ratepayers covered 82% of the cost of auditing the whole town's books. Professional Management Solutions, the consulting firm hired in April 2023 at $155 an hour to clean up the town's broken 2022 records, billed $46,178 in 2024, 70% of it charged to the utility funds. And the full price of that cleanup cannot be assembled from public records at all: the Board packets for 2023, the year the cleanup ran, contain no check registers (registers appear in roughly two dozen of the 120 packets published since January 2023). What the ledger does show is the audit expense line sitting at $13 town-wide in 2023 while the delayed audits piled up, then $49,292 landing in 2024 as they were caught up — most of it, again, on the rate-funded side.

Put these paragraphs together and the shape is unmistakable: the rate money was raised for pipes; the pipes keep sliding right; and the margin the delays create is being consumed by overhead, equipment, and payroll — routed into the rate-funded side of the ledger by an allocation formula no one has published. A budget deficit that would be visible in the General Fund has been relocated into future water bills, where it will surface not as a deficit line but as the next rate increase.

Chapter Ten

If 2027–2029 look like 2008–2010

None of the arithmetic above assumes a bad economy. Now assume one — a recession on the scale of 2008–2010, applied to the revenue base the audit just certified. This is an illustration on stated assumptions, not a forecast; the assumptions are printed so anyone can rerun them. Against that gap the town holds $869,067 of unassigned General Fund reserve. The TABOR emergency reserve ($69,500) legally cannot be used for revenue shortfalls, and the capital fund's million-plus balance legally cannot pay salaries. At a $416,000 annual gap the unassigned reserve is exhausted partway through the third year — with every operating cost frozen at today’s level — and public safety is half of General Fund spending, so cuts of that magnitude mean cutting the police department, because there is nowhere else large enough to cut. Paonia survived worse shocks before: the mine closures of 2013–2016 took 359 jobs out of a town of 1,450, and the Town rode it out on a $387,311 fund balance. But that town carried roughly $938,000 of payroll. This one carries $2.3 million. The same storm hits a much bigger sail.

And the table above is generous, because it holds costs flat. Costs are not flat. The town's own budgets set the escalators: every employee received a minimum 5.7% cost-of-living raise going into 2025, the FY-2026 budget carries 2–3% COLA plus 2–3% merit on top, health insurance premiums rose 13% in one year — on a plan where the town pays 90% of the premium — and the payroll base is $2.3 million town-wide. Personnel costs compounding at even 5% add roughly $115,000 of new annual cost every year, recession or not. Layer that on the revenue shock and the annual gap reaches $500,000–$600,000 by the third year; the two-and-a-half-year runway shortens to well under two.

On pensions specifically, the exposure is real but asymmetric. General employees are on a defined-contribution 401(k) — market losses there hit employees' accounts, not the town's budget. The police, however, are in FPPA's Statewide Defined Benefit Plan. Today that plan is the town's quietest piece of good news: the audit reports a net pension liability of zero, with the town holding a proportionate share of a net pension asset — the plan is overfunded as of the December 2024 measurement, and the town's contribution runs 8% of a $218,235 covered police payroll. And the flip is not hypothetical: the audit's own ten-year schedule shows Paonia's share turning into a liability twice in the past decade from ordinary market years — $5,871 in 2016 and $23,649 in 2018 (13.9% of covered police payroll), each reversed when markets recovered. Those were single soft years against the plan's assumed 7.0% return. A 2008-scale event — deep losses sustained across two or three years — would, by the same proportional arithmetic, put a liability in the low-to-mid six figures on the town's balance sheet and push employer contribution rates up statewide for years, the way the 2008 crash forced FPPA members to ratchet their own contributions from 8% to 12% of salary between 2014 and 2022. And the plan's own methods — five-year asset smoothing, thirty-year open amortization — mean the bill arrives two to five years after the crash and persists: precisely when sales tax and interest income are still down. It is not the biggest number in a downturn scenario; it is the one that arrives last and stays longest.

The utility side is where a recession does structural damage. Debt service on $9.67 million does not shrink when the economy does. Delinquencies rise; the state loans carry coverage requirements that rates must satisfy; and the customer base legally cannot grow while the moratorium holds. Every stress path on the rate-funded side of the ledger resolves the same way: further rate increases, on ratepayers whose median household income already qualifies the town as a state-designated Disadvantaged Community — and who are now also carrying the administration's payroll through the allocation formula.

Which leads to the third outcome, and it is the one the incentives now point to. A town whose fixed costs have outgrown a frozen revenue base has exactly one lever that raises revenue without a vote to cut anything: growth. New water taps mean tap fees, new base charges, new construction — and under TABOR, new construction is the only thing that raises the town's revenue ceiling. The town's own financial report already anticipates the turn: "once conditions of the moratorium are met… these limiting factors may be reduced." When the reserve runs low and the rate increases grow politically impossible, expect the moratorium's end — and the development that follows — to be presented not as a choice but as a fiscal necessity: we need the growth to pay for the government we already built. That is the oldest bailout in municipal finance, and the arithmetic for it is being assembled now. The original promise ran the other way: fix the water system first, then grow when it is safe. Whether growth happens on the infrastructure's schedule or on the budget's schedule is the decision this document exists to put in front of the town.

Chapter Eleven

Seven questions with no good answers

Every load-bearing fact in this document comes from the town's own records — its adopted budgets, its audited report's own narrative, its Board's own minutes and meeting videos. Nothing here rests on an allegation. What remains are seven questions the town has not been asked on the record, and has never answered:

The methodology question

1. "What would the 2025 General Fund result have been under the 2024 allocation methodology?"

This is the single most important question. It converts the reallocation from a footnote into a number, and the answer is roughly negative $220,000. If staff can't produce it, that itself is the finding.

The transparency question

2. "Is the new cost-allocation formula documented, and did the Board ever approve it?"

A change this consequential — it swung the town's headline number by more than $300,000 — should exist as a written policy the Board adopted, not an administrative choice discovered after the fact in an audit narrative.

The ratepayer question

3. "How much administrative overhead do the Water, Sewer, and Trash funds now carry, and is it reflected in the five-year rate plans and the loan coverage requirements?"

The state loans come with covenants — promises that rate revenue will exceed debt payments by a margin. New overhead eats that margin. Someone should confirm the rate plan still works with the costs that just moved in.

The sustainability question

4. "What does the five-year projection model show for the General Fund, and can the public see it?"

The audited report says the town runs a five-year forecast every year. It's a public record. Either it shows the structural gap widening or it doesn't — and either answer moves the conversation forward.

The windfall question

5. "What investment income is the town assuming for 2026 and 2027 as interest rates fall?"

Nearly 11% of General Fund revenue is interest earnings the audit itself warns will decline. If the budget assumes that money persists, the real gap is bigger than the stated one.

The governance question

6. "Mr. Sund told this Board on June 23 he expected the audit to flag separation of duties, and that he'd propose an accounting position in the 2027 budget. The audit didn't flag it. Is the position still going in the budget — and will the next administrator's job description separate the Treasurer role?"

This ties everything together and asks the Board to act on their own staff's advice at the exact moment the org chart is being redrawn — while closing the escape hatch of "the audit didn't require it." It is the hardest question in this list to answer with "no."

The disclosure question

7. "When the Board approved Resolution 30-2025 amending the 2025 budget on December 30, why was the sanitation overrun attributed to vehicle maintenance when the fund's salary line was $21,000 over budget — and why has the allocation shift never been named in any hearing?"

The town's audited report states no interfund transfers occurred in 2025 — the allocation runs as direct payroll charges, the mechanism state law does not require a public resolution to move. The year-end budget amendment was properly noticed and adopted, and its line detail quietly recorded the utility admin actuals. But the presentation to the Board named grants, late invoices, and "sanitation fund overruns related to vehicle maintenance." Procedural compliance was perfect. The one thing never said out loud, in three years of budget hearings, is what was moving and why.

The whole story in one paragraph. The audit is clean, and the books are honest. What the honest books say is this: revenue is flat, and has been for years. The General Fund "improved" in 2025 only because administrative and governing-body costs were moved onto the water and sewer funds through an unpublished formula, and because of interest earnings that are already shrinking. Measured the way 2024 was measured, 2025 was another deficit year — and the town's own adopted budget commits, in writing, to spending savings on operations again in 2026. The costs that moved now sit on utility funds carrying the largest debt in town history, repaid by ratepayers who legally cannot grow in number and who — outside town limits — cannot vote for the Board that sets their rates. The rate increases were sold as infrastructure; the infrastructure keeps slipping; the spending arrives anyway. The town's own interim administrator has said the finance function needs real separation of duties. The record above says he is right.

What would fix it: a citizen-initiated ordinance now published at townofpaonia.co/petition — the Ratepayer Protection Ordinance, which ends the hidden formula and gives ratepayers a board that approves the allocation before it touches a bill.
